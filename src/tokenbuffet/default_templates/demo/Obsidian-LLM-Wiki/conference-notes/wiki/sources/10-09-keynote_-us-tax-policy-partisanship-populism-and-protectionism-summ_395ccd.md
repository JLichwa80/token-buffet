## Core Content

This keynote presentation delivers an empirical analysis of United States fiscal trajectory, focusing on how federal policy is shaped by the converging pressures of [[concepts/partisanship-populism-and-protectionism|partisanship, populism, and protectionism]]. Presented by a representative of the [[entities/tax-foundation|Tax Foundation]], the talk demonstrates the necessity of multi-year macroeconomic modeling and policymaker education in advancing structural, pro-growth tax policies rather than short-term political adjustments. 

Using historical case studies from the [[entities/tax-cuts-and-jobs-act|Tax Cuts and Jobs Act]], the speaker highlights key interventions involving leaders like [[entities/paul-ryan|Paul Ryan]], [[entities/kevin-brady|Kevin Brady]], and [[entities/steve-daines|Steve Daines]] to secure policy stability, particularly regarding the [[concepts/permanency|full deductibility of R&D and capital investment]]. The address warns of [[concepts/shrinking-tax-base|shrinking tax base dynamics]] fueled by bipartisan populist carveouts, escalating debt service costs consuming one-fifth of federal revenue, and emergent [[concepts/state-level-tax-experiments|state-level tax experiments]] that foreshadow national fiscal risks.

## Main Points

- US tax policy is increasingly governed by three converging forces: Partisanship (party-line voting), Populism (simplistic messaging over sound economics), and Protectionism (tariffs).
- Effective pro-growth tax reform centers on boosting long-term capital formation and labor supply, requiring policy permanency rather than temporary rate adjustments.
- Major legislative breakthroughs, such as securing permanent R&D expensing, depend on multi-year intellectual groundwork and macroeconomic modeling conducted before bills enter truncated legislative cycles.
- Bipartisan populist proposals (such as "no tax on" carveouts and wealth taxes) systematically erode the tax base and concentrate the fiscal burden onto a narrow band of taxpayers.
- Escalating federal debt has driven net interest obligations to absorb 20% of tax revenue, accelerating a fiscal "doom loop" that constrains future policy flexibility.
