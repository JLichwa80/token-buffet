# Cap-weighted indexing

## Definition

Cap-weighted indexing is a conventional passive portfolio construction methodology in which underlying securities are weighted in proportion to their total market capitalization. Rather than remaining entirely passive, the marginal turnover required to track changing market values systematically buys companies as they appreciate and trims them as they decline.

## Key Characteristics

- **Market Capitalization Proportionality**: Positions scale directly with market valuation, meaning larger companies occupy larger portfolio weights.
- **Predominantly Buy-and-Hold**: Approximately 95% of the portfolio operates as a static buy-and-hold allocation with minimal turnover (typically around 5% annually).
- **Procyclical Marginal Turnover**: The marginal 5% of traded turnover tends to purchase expanding, growth-oriented companies at premium multiples while divesting from discounted or depreciating value companies.
- **Concentration Episodes**: Prone to thematic ballooning where sectors or countries reach disproportionate weights relative to their fundamental economic footprint (e.g., technology during dot-com and AI cycles, or Japan representing nearly half of global market cap during the late 1980s).
- **Macroeconomic Disconnect**: Does not naturally anchor portfolio weights to broader macroeconomic or fundamental output, creating exposure to speculative pricing.

## Applications

- **Standard Benchmark Tracking**: Serves as the primary baseline archetype for core market index funds and exchange-traded funds (e.g., S&P 500, MSCI World).
- **Passive Core Allocation**: Deployed by institutional and retail investors seeking low transaction turnover, liquidity, and broad beta exposure.
- **Counter-Reference for Smart Beta**: Acts as the structural benchmark against which alternative methodologies—such as fundamental weighting or equal weighting—seek to capture excess rebalancing return.
