# Idiosyncratic Risk

## Definition

Idiosyncratic Risk refers to the company- or asset-specific exposure borne by an investor holding a concentrated position in a single stock or private enterprise. Unlike systematic market risk, idiosyncratic risk cannot be diversified away without reducing exposure to the specific holding, which in wealth management often creates tension between downside portfolio volatility and immediate tax liability.

## Key Characteristics

- **Firm-Specific Exposure**: Stemming directly from the operational, governance, and financial variables of an individual company rather than macroeconomic factors.
- **Diversification Hurdle & Tax Drag**: Eliminating idiosyncratic risk through asset sales triggers immediate capital gains realization, necessitating strategic tax-loss harvesting or gradual wind-down structures.
- **Behavioral & Emotional Friction**: Often coupled with executive or founder bias, where personal loyalty and familiarity lead to resistance against portfolio rebalancing.
- **Asymmetric Downside**: Concentrates catastrophic drawdown risk in a single asset without compensatory excess return expectations relative to an efficient, diversified portfolio.

## Applications

- **Concentrated Wealth Transition**: Designing staged, multi-year diversification roadmaps that balance risk mitigation targets against annual realized tax brackets.
- **Executive Compensation Management**: Structuring hedging strategies, exchange funds, and scheduled liquidations (e.g., 10b5-1 plans) for corporate insiders.
- **After-Tax Wealth Optimization**: Quantifying the threshold where the mathematical cost of portfolio volatility outweighs the frictional tax drag of selling concentrated holdings.
