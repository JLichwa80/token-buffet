# Cobra Effect

## Definition

The Cobra Effect is a metaphor for a policy incentive that increases the problem it was meant to reduce. The image is a bounty on rat tails, or cobras, that leads people to breed more of the animal. In tax policy, the same pattern appears when a narrowly aimed rule later reaches a much wider set of taxpayers. The talk uses the alternative minimum tax as the example: it was designed for 155 wealthy families and later hit far more people. The practical lesson is to judge second-order effects and not to treat a policy fix as permanent or precisely targeted.

## Key Characteristics

- An incentive changes behavior in the direction opposite to the stated goal.
- The historical image is a bounty that encourages breeding of the targeted animal.
- The alternative minimum tax is the tax-policy illustration: a rule aimed at 155 wealthy families that later reached many more taxpayers.
- A provision can drift from its original targets and still remain in force.
- Second-order effects matter more than the first-order intent of the rule.
- The phrase is one of the talk’s recurring catchphrases, alongside the spider’s web of acronyms.

## Applications

- Reading the alternative minimum tax as a rule whose reach outgrew its original target of 155 wealthy families.
- Testing a tax change for who will adapt, breed the rewarded behavior, or fall inside a wider net.
- Designing wrappers that still work when incentives twist and when provisions sunset, rather than assuming a targeted fix stays fixed.
- Treating the tax code’s accumulated acronyms as a place where good intentions and later behavior diverge.
