# Populism

## Definition

Populism in the context of tax policy refers to the rise of simplified, politically expedient, and vote-ready tax proposals driven by political rhetoric rather than modeled economic efficiency or pro-growth reform. It operates across both major political parties, leveraging simplistic messaging to gain electoral appeal in legislative environments characterized by limited technical tax policy expertise.

## Key Characteristics

- **Bipartisan Appeal:** Manifests across the ideological spectrum, spanning left-wing proposals (e.g., surtaxes and wealth taxes) and right-wing proposals (e.g., categorical exemptions such as "no tax on tips, overtime, or senior income").
- **Erosion of the Tax Base:** Relies on targeted exemptions, deductions, and carve-outs that narrow the overall tax base over time.
- **Concentration of Tax Burden:** Accelerates distributional shifts where a progressively smaller cohort of high earners bears a disproportionate share of total income tax revenue.
- **Opposition to Modeled Reform:** Contrasts directly with comprehensive, modeled pro-growth tax restructuring that emphasizes base-broadening and rate reduction.

## Applications

- **Electoral Platform Design:** Packaging tax exemptions into high-visibility campaign pledges to attract targeted voting demographics.
- **Fiscal Policy Analysis:** Evaluating the structural and revenue impacts of base-narrowing tax legislation on long-term government deficits and tax burden distribution.
- **Legislative Strategy:** Analyzing the political dynamics and vote-mobilization incentives that lead lawmakers to bypass traditional tax committee scrutiny in favor of populist measures.
