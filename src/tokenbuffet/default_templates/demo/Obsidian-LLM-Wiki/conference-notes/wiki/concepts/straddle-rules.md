# Straddle Rules

## Definition

Straddle rules are the tax constraint the panel uses to explain why a conventional collar on stock can be a poor after-tax hedge. For post-1984 stock, the call premium on a separable collar is currently taxable as short-term gain, while losses on the put are non-deductible and are added to the share basis until the stock is sold. The discussion treats this as practitioner nuance in a comparison of variable prepaid forwards, short box spreads, and collars, not as a full restatement of the statute.

## Key Characteristics

- On a separable collar over post-1984 stock, the call premium is taxed currently as short-term gain.
- Put losses on that collar are non-deductible and are added to the share basis until the stock is sold.
- That timing asymmetry is why the summary calls vanilla exchange-traded collars tax-inefficient.
- The stated remedy is a single OTC contract that fuses the put and the call so they cannot be separated, then borrowing against the present value of the put.
- That fused structure is described as mechanically akin to a [[Variable Prepaid Forward]].
- The rules appear as market-practitioner detail in the Q&A, not as a statutory treatise.

## Applications

Advisors use the rules when choosing how to hedge a concentrated stock position. A listed collar that keeps the put and the call separable can create current short-term income on the call while deferring any tax benefit of the put until the shares are sold. Where a collar is still the right hedge, the panel favors one OTC instrument that fuses both options, with borrowing against the present value of the put, or a [[Variable Prepaid Forward]] when certainty of outcome and constructive-sale management matter. A [[Box spread]] remains the comparison case for fixed-rate borrowing when no downside hedge is required.
