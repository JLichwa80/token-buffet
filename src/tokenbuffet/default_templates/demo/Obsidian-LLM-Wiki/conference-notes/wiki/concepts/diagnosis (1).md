# Diagnosis

## Definition

Diagnosis is the opening stage of after-tax portfolio design. Before an advisor recommends or builds a portfolio, the advisor maps the client’s household structure: linked accounts, tax statuses, embedded gains, legacy positions, risk exposures, and liquidity needs across the family balance sheet. The aim is to locate embedded [[Tax Drag]] and set a custom transition path, rather than start from a generic asset model.

## Key Characteristics

- It comes before design and implementation. The advisor first establishes the client’s actual household structure.
- The unit of analysis is the full family balance sheet, including taxable accounts, IRAs, trusts, 529s, donor-advised funds, concentrated stock, business interests, and legacy fund positions.
- Asset location by tax status (taxable, tax-deferred, tax-exempt) is treated as known. The harder work is coordination across households and linked accounts.
- Success is judged by after-tax outcomes, not by how a pre-tax model or benchmark looks.
- It replaces the product sequence of forecast, best-in-class product, quarterly rebalance, and one-size-fits-all delivery with a household view, an intended destination, and tax-lot-level customization.
- It treats [[Tax Drag]] as several layered problems — pooled-product distributions, cash lock-ins, realized gains and losses, portfolio risk, and family-level outcomes — and asks what to infer, what to realize, how to offset, and what trade-offs the client will accept.

## Applications

- Open a taxable relationship by building a household balance sheet view and marking where tax drag is embedded before any product is selected.
- Run a [[Tax-Aware Transition Analysis]] for legacy mutual funds or other commingled positions so the client can compare staying put with moving toward a destination portfolio.
- Size transition, diversification, or tax-aware long-short work against current-year gains, available losses, liquidity events, and the risk change that selling, holding, or hedging would create.
- Coordinate the advisor, CPA, and estate counsel around one family-level picture when concentrated stock, a business sale, or several account types sit on the same balance sheet.
