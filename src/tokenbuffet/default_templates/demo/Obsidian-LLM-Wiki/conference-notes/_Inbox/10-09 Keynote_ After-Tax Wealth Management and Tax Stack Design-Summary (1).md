# at this tax conference Keynote: After-Tax Wealth Management and Tax Stack Design
## Main Theme & Core Message
The keynote’s central theme was the evolution of wealth management from **pre-tax product selection** to **after-tax portfolio design**. Aaron’s presentation argued that the future of taxable wealth is not about choosing a model, selecting a product, and rebalancing quarterly. It is about building an **after-tax implementation platform** — what he called a **“tax stack”** — that allows advisors to diagnose tax drag, design around the family balance sheet, and implement client-specific portfolios at the tax-lot level.
His core message was direct: **taxable wealth is different**. Clients do not live in pre-tax returns; they live in after-tax outcomes. The industry was built around pre-tax portfolios, one-size-fits-all models, and broad product selection, but high-net-worth clients arrive with concentrated stock, legacy mutual funds, donor-advised funds, trusts, 529s, IRAs, business interests, and pending liquidity events. These are not product problems. They are design problems. As Aaron closed: **“The future of taxable wealth is designed; it’s not defaulted.”**
This message matters now because technology has finally made it possible to do what advisors have long known was necessary: manage wealth across households, accounts, tax statuses, asset classes, liquidity needs, risk budgets, gain budgets, and transition paths. The timeless insight is that **tax drag is not one problem — it is many problems layered together**. The advisor’s job is no longer simply to recommend investments. The advisor becomes the **after-tax architect**, coordinating CPA, estate counsel, legal counsel, client behavior, and portfolio implementation into one integrated design.
## Structured Breakdown: Chapters & Subthemes
## 1. The Opening Scenario: The Prospect With a Complicated Balance Sheet
Aaron opened by asking the audience to imagine a very specific moment: it is **9:00 a.m. Eastern on a Thursday**, someone is watching CNBC, trying to figure out whether the market narrative is “Taco Thursday,” “Turnaround Thursday,” or whatever headline just came across their smartphone — and suddenly a prospect walks into the office.
That prospect is not simple. They are in a **concentrated position in a private business**. They have **legacy mutual funds** purchased around 2008. They have a **DAF**, **trusts**, **529s**, **IRAs**, and multiple other accounts and planning structures.
The point of the story was to ground the entire talk in the actual complexity advisors face. The problem is no longer, “Which model should this client own?” The problem is, “How do we design across the entire household balance sheet in a way that produces better after-tax outcomes?”
Aaron framed this as the shift from the old paradigm to the new one:
- From **product-focused mentality**
- From **model-focused mentality**
- Toward an **after-tax implementation platform**
- Toward a full **tax stack**, not just a tech stack
He introduced two foundational terms:
### Tax Drag
**Tax drag** is the difference between the pre-tax return of an asset or portfolio and the after-tax return the client actually experiences.
It is the hidden cost that quietly erodes wealth when portfolios are managed without tax awareness.
### Tax Design
**Tax design** is the deliberate, proactive, engineered approach to minimizing tax drag and improving family-level outcomes.
This is where Aaron believes the entire industry is moving: from tax being an afterthought to tax being part of the architecture.
## 2. Diagnosis: Understanding the Real Problem Before Designing the Portfolio
Aaron described the first stage as **diagnosis**. Before advisors can design anything, they need to understand the client’s actual household structure.
He explained that the difficult allocation work is not just asset allocation in the abstract. Advisors already know, at least intuitively, which kinds of assets belong in taxable accounts, tax-deferred accounts, or tax-exempt accounts. The real complication appears when the advisor has to look **across households and linked accounts**.
The core diagnostic question becomes:
> How do we look across the full family balance sheet?
This includes:
- Taxable accounts
- IRAs
- Trusts
- 529s
- Donor-advised funds
- Concentrated stock
- Business interests
- Legacy fund positions
- Embedded gains
- Client liquidity needs
- Tax status of each account
- Risk exposure across the household
Aaron emphasized that the tax code, the S&P, MSCI benchmarks, and account structures all need to be judged by **after-tax outcomes**, not pre-tax portfolio appearances.
He contrasted the old industry methodology:
1. Build a model from capital market forecasts.
2. Select a best-in-class product.
3. Offer that product to the client.
4. Rebalance quarterly.
5. Apply it as a one-size-fits-all solution.
Against the new client expectation:
1. Look at the household balance sheet.
2. Design with intent and destination in mind.
3. Judge success by after-tax outcomes.
4. Monitor continuously.
5. Automate execution.
6. Customize at the tax-lot level.
The key argument was that clients can already build simple ETF portfolios themselves. Advisors create differentiated value by solving the problems clients cannot easily solve alone: tax drag, transitions, concentrated positions, account coordination, liquidity needs, and household-level implementation.
## 3. Tax Drag Is Not One Problem — It Is Five Problems
Aaron made an important distinction: **tax drag is not a single problem**. It is a collection of problems that compound into complexity.
He referenced several sources of tax drag, including:
### 1. Pooled Products
Mutual funds and other commingled products can generate unpredictable taxable distributions. The investor may owe taxes even if they did not sell anything personally.
### 2. Cash Lock-Ins
Clients often hold appreciated positions because selling would trigger taxes. This creates “locked” capital that may no longer be optimal from a risk or allocation perspective.
### 3. Realized Gains and Losses
Advisors must understand what gains are being realized, what losses are available, and how they can be paired.
### 4. Portfolio Risk
Tax decisions are never purely tax decisions. Selling, holding, transitioning, or hedging all change the risk profile.
### 5. Family-Level Outcomes
The household outcome matters more than any individual account’s appearance. The real question is whether the overall family balance sheet is moving toward a better after-tax, risk-aware destination.
This diagnostic section established the need for a decision framework: advisors must understand what to infer, what to realize, how to offset, and how to keep the client comfortable with the trade-offs.
## 4. Design: Building the “Tax Stack”
After diagnosis, Aaron moved into **design**.
He introduced the idea of the **tax stack** as parallel to the familiar **tech stack**. Advisors already think about their CRM, planning software, reporting tools, custodial integrations, and portfolio systems as a technology stack. Aaron argued they must now think just as deliberately about their tax stack.
The tax stack includes:
- A **household tax plan**
- A view of the **family balance sheet**
- Awareness of different **tax statuses**
- Asset class placement
- Asset location
- Tax-lot-level implementation
- Direct indexing
- Transition management
- Concentrated stock management
- Liquidity event planning
- Governance and reporting
The real power, according to Aaron, lies in the **implementation layer**.
This is where technology creates value because it enables:
- Lot-level optimization
- Direct indexing
- Tax-aware transitions
- Concentrated stock unwinds
- Harvesting losses
- Pairing losses against gains
- Managing around client-specific restrictions
- Building custom portfolios around a client’s existing holdings
Aaron was clear that modern transition management is not about “ripping the Band-Aid off.” It is not merely about staging sales over time. It is about designing a path — understanding exposure, risk, taxes, losses, gains, and destination.
## 5. The Importance of Governance and Reporting
Aaron emphasized that implementation without governance is not enough.
When advisors present a tax-aware transition analysis to a client, the numbers can hit hard. A client may suddenly realize:
- The tax cost of transition
- The risk of staying where they are
- The cost of moving too fast
- The cost of moving too slowly
- The trade-offs between current taxes and future benefits
This is why reporting and governance are “unbelievably important.” Clients need to see the roadmap, understand the trade-offs, and feel that decisions are being made deliberately.
Governance turns tax-aware implementation from a black box into a client conversation.
## 6. Five Principles for Designing Passive Tax-Loss Strategies
Aaron then proposed **five principles** for thinking about the design of passive tax-loss strategies.
## Principle 1: Own Securities Where Control Matters
The first principle is to **own securities where control matters**.
Owning underlying securities instead of pooled products gives advisors control over:
- Tax-loss harvesting
- Client restrictions
- Concentrated positions
- Cash-flow needs
- Factor exposure
- Active or passive preferences
- Income orientation
- Defensive orientation
- Blackout windows for executives
- Year-specific liquidity requirements
The point is not that every portfolio must own every security directly. The point is that advisors should take control where control creates client value.
Aaron emphasized that direct ownership allows the portfolio to **bend around the client’s needs**.
## Principle 2: Losses Are Inventory
The second principle was one of the talk’s most memorable ideas: **losses are inventory**.
Tax-loss harvesting is often discussed mechanically, but Aaron reframed it more powerfully. Inventorying losses is a way of **monetizing volatility**.
Volatility is usually treated as something clients dislike or fear. But in a tax-aware framework, volatility can be harvested and converted into a tax asset.
Losses can be used to:
- Offset current gains
- Prepare for future gains
- Fund transitions
- Support liquidity events
- Create flexibility in the family balance sheet
Aaron noted that loss generation happens not only when the whole market is down, but also through **cross-sectional dispersion** — when individual securities move differently within the market.
## Principle 3: Use Budgets, Not Gaps
The third principle was: **use budgets, not gaps**.
Aaron argued that most high-net-worth clients, with guidance from advisors and accountants, should have a reasonably good sense of what their current-year tax picture looks like. Therefore, there is “not a ton of excuse” for failing to have proactive conversations at the beginning of the year.
He highlighted several kinds of budgets:
### Gain Budgets
How much gain can the client tolerate this year?
This might be zero, or it might be a positive amount if prior losses are available.
### Risk Budgets
How sensitive should the portfolio be to the target investment model?
How much tracking error or model drift is acceptable?
### Time Budgets
How quickly should the portfolio transition from its current state to the destination portfolio?
### Liquidity Budgets
What cash needs does the client have this year?
This was especially important because Aaron noted that many academic studies on tax-loss harvesting assume no cash flows. In real life, that assumption is false.
Across the platform Aaron referenced — managing approximately **$33 billion** across **16,000 accounts** — he said roughly **60% to 70% of individual accounts** have some kind of cash flow in a given year, either in or out.
That makes tax-aware deposits and withdrawals extremely powerful.
## Principle 4: Transitions Are Roadmaps
The fourth principle was that **transitions are roadmaps**.
In the old product-centric model, transition meant either:
- Sell everything immediately and realize the tax cost, or
- Stage sales over time.
Aaron argued that this is too simplistic. Modern transition management should include:
1. Mapping existing exposures.
2. Comparing them to the target portfolio.
3. Harvesting at the tax-lot level.
4. Pairing harvested losses against gains.
5. Moving gradually toward the destination.
6. Rebuilding the portfolio with intent.
The key phrase was that it is no longer just about **sell or hold**. It is about the full life cycle of designing the transition.
## Principle 5: Concentrated Wealth Is a Different Version of the Same Problem
The fifth principle focused on concentrated wealth.
Concentrated wealth brings all the same tax issues as other transitions, but adds a major layer: **idiosyncratic risk**.
If a client owns a large single-stock position, the challenge is not only tax cost. It is also the behavioral attachment and the risk of holding too much of one company.
Modern approaches can include:
- Stage sales
- Managed options
- Direct indexing around the position
- Tax-aware long-short strategies
- Risk-managed wind-downs
- Tax-deferred structuring
- Custom portfolio construction around the concentrated holding
The advisor must handle the interaction between taxes, risk, and client psychology.
## 7. Applications: Where Tax Design Creates Value
Aaron then moved into practical applications.
## Application 1: The Ossified Portfolio
The **ossified portfolio** is one that is not broken enough to sell, but too tax-burdened to easily fix.
This often appears in legacy commingled funds or mutual funds with embedded gains. Clients may not love the portfolio, but selling creates a tax bill they do not want.
Aaron explained that commingled funds are especially difficult because they require a look-through process that is complex both from an optimization standpoint and a technology infrastructure standpoint.
The first step is a **tax-aware transition analysis**, so the client can understand the trade-offs between staying put and moving toward a better destination.
## Application 2: The Concentrated Executive
The next application was the **concentrated executive**.
Aaron described a familiar scenario: an executive has worked at a company for 20 or 30 years, accumulated large stock positions or options, and now faces the question of what to do with all that concentrated wealth.
The advisor can show charts:
- Top 10 companies from 20 years ago versus today
- Volatility of a single stock versus a diversified portfolio
But Aaron warned that the client’s eyes may glaze over because they believe:
> “Yeah, but not my company. I helped build this.”
This was one of the most human parts of the talk. The challenge is not just mathematical; it is emotional. The executive believes their company is different.
Aaron suggested that instead of forcing the issue, advisors can present tax-aware diversification options and ask whether the client would be willing to diversify some portion if it can be done intelligently and tax-sensitively. The goal is to let the client “dip their toe into it.”
## Application 3: The Pending Liquidity Event
Aaron described this as “unbelievably powerful.”
Many advisors work with business owners, and the market is seeing both public-market liquidity events and generational transitions across small and midsized businesses.
The planning opportunity is to allocate assets in advance of a business sale in order to generate losses that can offset the large capital gain from the liquidity event.
Tools may include:
- Long-only direct indexing
- Tax-loss harvesting portfolios
- Tax-aware long-short strategies
- More aggressive loss-generation tools
The deeper advisor value is not just tax minimization; it is getting into the psychology, timeline, and personal planning needs of the client before the event occurs.
## Application 4: The Taxable Income Portfolio
Aaron called this one of the most tried-and-true ways to manage high-net-worth income: **municipal bonds**.
He acknowledged that municipal bonds are not exciting — “there is nothing sexy about this” — but emphasized that they are extremely powerful, particularly because of their potential **triple tax advantage**.
He also described the power of pairing municipal bonds with a direct index in the same account, allowing customization by:
- Duration
- Quality exposure
- State-specific preferences
- Federally preferred income
- Client tax profile
The point was that even “boring” tools can become powerful when placed inside a broader tax design.
## Application 5: Tax-Aware Long-Short
Aaron described tax-aware long-short strategies as a utility tool for solving some of the thorniest client problems.
He offered four questions advisors should ask before using such a strategy:
### 1. Are You Comfortable With the Alpha Thesis?
These are active strategies, and advisors must underwrite the investment thesis, including risks around short exposure.
### 2. Is There a Use for the Losses?
Generating losses is only valuable if the client has a plan for them.
Aaron’s challenge was direct: if the client has no use for the losses, what is the point of paying the fees?
### 3. Is the Client Comfortable With Complexity?
Some clients will never be comfortable with gross exposure above 100%, shorting, leverage, or the complexity of the structure.
### 4. Is the Client Comfortable Restructuring the Balance Sheet?
Tax-aware long-short can require a major restructuring of the household’s equity exposure. Aaron noted that shorting against the box can trigger onerous consequences if not handled correctly, including disallowed losses.
Therefore, the strategy may need to become the client’s core U.S. equity or global equity exposure rather than a small isolated sleeve.
## 8. The Payout: Hundreds of Basis Points of After-Tax Excess Return
Aaron called this the most important slide in the deck — joking that the audience could tell because it had all the disclaimers at the bottom.
The payoff for all this design, planning, and implementation is tangible: the potential to generate **hundreds of basis points of after-tax excess return**.
He framed this in information-ratio terms, noting that if the after-tax benefit is reliable, the trade can be very attractive.
He cited several general ranges:
- ETF and fund models: approximately **50 to 100 basis points** in tax alpha
- Direct and custom indexes: approximately **15 to 200 basis points**
- Active models with overlays: meaningful tax alpha when tax overlay is added
- Municipal bonds: enhancements through tax-preferred income
- Tax-aware long-short: **300+ basis points** on a 130/30 strategy, with more potential as leverage increases
The key lesson: tax design is not cosmetic. It can create measurable, client-visible value.
## 9. Institutionalizing Tax Design Across Advisor Practices
Aaron closed by explaining how advisor practices can institutionalize this approach.
He described a capabilities map:
1. Identify the client need.
2. Determine the required capabilities.
3. Build a solution inside the after-tax implementation ecosystem.
4. Automate as much as possible.
5. Allow the advisor to use judgment on which levers to pull.
He emphasized that tax-smart platforms contain many levers — gain budgets, risk budgets, liquidity budgets, time budgets — and although they may look like constraints, they are actually powerful conversation tools.
They force the advisor to act as the coordinator among:
- CPA
- Estate attorney
- Legal counsel
- Investment team
- Client
- Custodian
- Technology platform
Aaron then gave a behind-the-scenes operational example. Living on the East Coast and dealing with the three-hour time difference, he woke up around 4:00 a.m. and saw roughly 100 Slack messages from his team. Work had begun in Poland in the middle of the night, reconciling accounts and pulling custodial files across the 16,000 accounts they manage. Then the work moved to the East Coast team, loading lot files, reviewing short inventory, and preparing the automated implementation engine.
This story illustrated that automation is not magic. A tremendous amount of infrastructure exists behind the scenes so that advisors can focus on being the **after-tax architect**.
## 10. Closing Argument: Designed, Not Defaulted
Aaron’s final argument was crisp:
> “The future of taxable wealth is designed; it’s not defaulted.”
Default is what happens when advisors allow tax drag to remain embedded in portfolios. Design is the deliberate, proactive approach to the holistic family balance sheet.
He left the audience with three closing principles:
1. **Taxable wealth is different.**
2. **Control is incredibly valuable.**
3. **The advisor is central to everything.**
The phrase “my fellow tax nerds” captured the room’s shared identity: this was a technical audience, but also one that understood that better tax design is not merely technical — it is central to client outcomes.
## 11. Transition to Rob Arnott: “Is Your Alpha Big Enough to Cover Its Taxes?”
After Aaron’s presentation, the next speaker introduced a resource for attendees: a QR code in their tote bags linking to a special collaboration with PMR and articles from the archives. One highlighted article was from **1993**, titled **“Is Your Alpha Big Enough to Cover Its Taxes?”**
Rob Arnott, founder and chairman of Research Affiliates, then came to the stage.
Rob began by thanking the audience and reflecting on the 1993 paper he wrote with Tad Jordanrey. He said the paper may have inadvertently helped kick off a revolution in tax-aware investing, though he did not claim credit for creating that revolution.
The original paper’s message was simple but profound: **management fees are not the only cost that matters. Taxes matter.**
He invoked Benjamin Franklin’s famous line:
> “The only things we can’t avoid in life are death and taxes.”
Rob then reviewed findings from the original work, which looked at 25 years of mutual fund returns across approximately 4,000 mutual funds tracked by Morningstar.
Key findings included:
- Before fees, the average fund produced returns about **20 basis points below its benchmark**.
- Net of fees, that became approximately **minus 1%**.
- Net of taxes, it became approximately **minus 2.5%**.
- After liquidation, it became approximately **minus 3% per annum**.
He tied this to Bill Sharpe’s arithmetic of active management: if index funds own the market, the remaining active managers collectively also own the market, before costs. Therefore, fees and taxes become decisive.
Rob’s portion reinforced Aaron’s message historically: tax-aware investing is not a fad. It has been a central but underappreciated problem for decades.

## Core Concepts & Learning Highlights
## 1. Tax Stack
The **tax stack** is the advisor’s tax-aware operating infrastructure. Just as firms build technology stacks for CRM, trading, reporting, and planning, they must build tax stacks for managing household-level after-tax outcomes.
It includes planning, account coordination, tax-lot management, direct indexing, transition analysis, tax-loss harvesting, concentrated stock management, and governance.
## 2. Tax Drag
**Tax drag** is the gap between pre-tax and after-tax returns. It is not just a cost line item; it is a structural wealth leak.
Aaron emphasized that tax drag appears through multiple channels: pooled product distributions, embedded gains, transition costs, cash-flow needs, concentration risk, and poor household coordination.
## 3. Tax Design
**Tax design** is the proactive architecture of taxable wealth. It moves tax from a backward-looking reporting issue to a forward-looking portfolio design issue.
Tax design asks: What is the client trying to accomplish, what taxes will be created, what losses can be used, what risks are acceptable, and how should the household balance sheet evolve?
## 4. Household Balance Sheet
The household balance sheet is the complete view of the family’s assets across accounts, tax statuses, entities, and goals.
This includes taxable accounts, retirement accounts, trusts, 529s, donor-advised funds, business interests, concentrated stock, and expected liquidity events.
The advisor must optimize across the household, not just within one account.
## 5. After-Tax Outcomes
Clients ultimately experience after-tax wealth. Pre-tax performance may look good in a report, but spendable, transferable, and usable wealth is after-tax.
This reframes the advisor’s success metric from beating a benchmark before taxes to improving the client’s real financial outcome after taxes.
## 6. Lot-Level Implementation
Lot-level implementation means making decisions at the individual tax-lot level rather than at the fund or model level.
This allows advisors to harvest losses, avoid unnecessary gains, manage transitions, and customize portfolios around client-specific needs.
## 7. Losses as Inventory
Losses are not merely accounting artifacts. They are assets that can be stored and used.
Loss inventory can offset current gains, future gains, business-sale gains, stock-sale gains, and transition costs.
## 8. Monetizing Volatility
Tax-loss harvesting turns volatility into an economic benefit. Market volatility and stock-level dispersion create opportunities to harvest losses without abandoning the overall investment exposure.
This reframes volatility from pure pain into potential tax value.
## 9. Budgets, Not Gaps
A tax-smart system should use explicit budgets:
- Gain budgets
- Risk budgets
- Time budgets
- Liquidity budgets
These budgets make the advisor’s process proactive rather than reactive.
## 10. Transitions as Roadmaps
A transition is not a binary choice between selling and holding. It is a roadmap from the current portfolio to the target portfolio.
The roadmap considers taxes, risk, time, liquidity, available losses, tracking error, and client psychology.
## 11. Concentrated Wealth Management
Concentrated stock or business wealth requires special handling because it involves both taxes and emotional attachment.
The client may understand diversification intellectually but resist it emotionally. Effective advisors provide options that let clients reduce risk gradually and tax-efficiently.
## 12. Tax-Aware Long-Short
Tax-aware long-short strategies can generate significant losses and potentially large after-tax benefits, but they require careful underwriting.
Advisors must evaluate alpha thesis, complexity tolerance, loss usage, short exposure, and household-level restructuring.
## 13. Municipal Bonds as Taxable Income Tools
Municipal bonds may be boring, but they remain powerful for high-net-worth taxable income planning.
Their value increases when paired with broader tax-aware portfolio construction and state-specific customization.
## 14. Automation Plus Advisor Judgment
Automation handles reconciliation, lot files, custodial data, short inventory, optimization, and execution infrastructure.
But the advisor remains central because only the advisor can understand client behavior, coordinate external professionals, and decide which levers to pull.
## 15. Taxes as a Historical Performance Problem
Rob Arnott’s opening reinforced that taxes have long been a major drag on active manager results. The 1993 paper showed that after fees, taxes, and liquidation, mutual fund results could trail benchmarks by roughly 3% per year.
The lesson: ignoring taxes can overwhelm the value of alpha.
## Action Points for Immediate Implementation
1. **Build your tax stack, not just your tech stack.**
   Because clients need after-tax implementation, not just portfolio reporting. As Aaron said, “We’ve got to think about our tax stack as well.”
2. **Start every taxable relationship with a household balance sheet diagnosis.**
   Look across taxable accounts, trusts, IRAs, 529s, DAFs, business interests, concentrated stock, and expected liquidity events because the real problem is rarely isolated to one account.
3. **Use explicit budgets for gains, risk, time, and liquidity.**
   Do this at the beginning of the year with the client and CPA because, as Aaron said, there is “not a ton of excuse” for failing to have proactive tax conversations.
4. **Treat harvested losses as inventory.**
   Build and track loss inventory because losses can offset gains, fund transitions, and prepare for future liquidity events.
5. **Turn transitions into roadmaps.**
   Do not default to “sell everything” or “stage sales.” Map current exposures, compare them to the destination, harvest losses, pair gains, and move deliberately.
6. **Present concentrated clients with options, not lectures.**
   Executives and founders may believe “not my company.” Help them dip their toe into diversification with tax-aware scenarios that respect both the math and the emotion.
7. **Make the advisor the after-tax architect.**
   Coordinate the CPA, estate attorney, legal counsel, investment team, and platform tools because the advisor is central to translating complex tax design into client action.
**First step Aaron would likely recommend:**
Begin with the **diagnosis**: build a complete household-level view and identify where tax drag is currently hiding.
