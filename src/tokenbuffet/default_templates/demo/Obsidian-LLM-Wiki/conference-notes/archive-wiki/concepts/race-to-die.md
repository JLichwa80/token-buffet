## Description
"Race to die" is a catchphrase capturing a perverse dynamic in U.S. tax and estate planning where the timing of an investor's death creates significant tax consequences. The phrase encapsulates the tension between long-term wealth accumulation and step-up basis rules—a cornerstone of the current tax code that resets an inherited asset's cost basis to fair market value at death, eliminating unrealized gains from taxation. This creates an absurd incentive structure where death itself becomes a financial event to be anticipated and planned around, rather than an incidental life occurrence. In the context of institutional investing and policy design, "race to die" exemplifies how fragmented, temporary tax rules create perverse incentives and artificial deadlines that complicate strategies to compound wealth over 20–30 years. Understanding this dynamic is central to designing intelligent wrappers and portfolio structures that allow investors to build long-term wealth without having tax policy timing work against them. The phrase also illustrates the broader gap between Wall Street and Washington—230 miles and an infinite number of misunderstandings—where exploitable arbitrage exists in the space between complicated, often temporary tax rules and portfolio innovation.
