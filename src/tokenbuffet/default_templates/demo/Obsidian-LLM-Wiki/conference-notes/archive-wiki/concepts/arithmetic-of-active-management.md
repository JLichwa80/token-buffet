## Description
The Arithmetic of Active Management is an economic principle formulated by [[Bill Sharpe]] establishing that, before costs, the return on the average actively managed dollar must equal the return on the average passively managed dollar because active managers collectively own the market. Consequently, active management operates as a zero-sum game before expenses and a negative-sum game after costs. In wealth management applications, [[Rob Arnott]] expanded this theorem beyond advisory and administrative fees to incorporate tax friction and trading turnover, demonstrating that taxes constitute an even larger performance hurdle that systematically widens active manager underperformance relative to benchmarks. Arnott's research showed that after fees, taxes, and liquidation, mutual fund results can trail benchmark indices by roughly 3% annually, proving that tax friction can entirely overwhelm generated alpha.
