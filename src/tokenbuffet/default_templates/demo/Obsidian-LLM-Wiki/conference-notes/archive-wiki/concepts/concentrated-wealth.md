# Concentrated Wealth
## Description
Concentrated wealth is a taxable account dominated by one or a few large stock positions, including pre-IPO shares, where selling would realize gains. It carries the same tax issues as other transitions and adds a major layer of idiosyncratic risk. More broadly, a concentrated position is a large holding in a single stock, illiquid asset, or liquidity-event asset that can leave a client exposed to both tax friction and uncompensated investment risk. The problem is the size of a winner, which is distinct from an ossified multi-name mess defined by the count of old lots, though years of taxable investing can leave ossified portfolios that also hold concentrated winners. A Section 351 exchange can trim that concentration by contributing the position into a diversified ETF without a current tax bill, subject to household tests that the largest position is at most 25% of contributed assets and the top five are at most 50%. Synthetic rebalancing is a second path: collar the position, borrow against it at synthetic rates, and diversify into other assets while the original gain stays deferred. For high-net-worth holders, that option-box borrow is compared with a traditional pledged asset line, with a cited example under 3% after tax.
Beyond public equities, the discipline also encompasses private business stakes and addresses the behavioral attachment common among founders and long-tenured executives. Practical management extends to phased liquidation schedules, custom direct indexing designed specifically around the restricted or legacy asset, and aggressive loss-harvesting overlays to offset gains realized during the unwind.
The keynote uses Riley's Wachovia holding as the central example of how concentrated wealth can become difficult to diversify when selling would trigger a major capital-gains bill. The downside hazard is illustrated by Wachovia's share price collapsing from $60 to $7 during the 2008 financial crisis, highlighting the severe capital loss clients face when concentration risk materializes. Advisors historically faced "two bad options" for emotionally attached holdings and business-sale proceeds: keep the risk or sell and absorb the tax cost. Modern tax-advantaged long-short strategies, especially tech-enabled tools associated with the Brevan Howard/Nuveen platform, are presented as a third path for tax-neutral transitions that de-risk the portfolio, defer or offset taxes, and move the client toward an ideal allocation. Sam's story about her grandfather, Herr Schmaltz, a WWII fighter pilot, reinforces the human cost of failing to solve concentrated-wealth problems tax-efficiently.
